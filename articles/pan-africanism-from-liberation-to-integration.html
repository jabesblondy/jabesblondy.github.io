From Liberation to Integration: The Historical Journey of Pan-Africanism

From early diasporic congresses and anti-colonial organising to the OAU and African Union, Pan-Africanism has continually asked how a connected people can shape a shared political and economic future.

By Jabes Blondy · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Pan-Africanism is not simply a slogan about continental unity. It is a historical movement of ideas, organising and political action that emerged across borders and across the African diaspora. Its story is a story of people confronting domination, asserting dignity and debating what freedom should make possible. Its unfinished question is whether political solidarity can be translated into institutions and economic relationships capable of improving everyday life.

An idea formed across borders

The origins of Pan-Africanism cannot be reduced to one meeting or one individual. Its intellectual roots reach into the experiences of enslavement, racial exclusion, colonial rule and the struggle for self-determination. African-descended intellectuals and organisers in the Caribbean, the Americas, Europe and Africa developed a language of shared identity and common political interests long before most African territories achieved independence.

The 1900 Pan-African Conference in London, convened by Henry Sylvester Williams, is widely recognised as a landmark in the organised movement. It helped establish a platform through which people of African descent could articulate common concerns and challenge racial injustice. Subsequent congresses in the early twentieth century expanded these networks. The movement was never entirely uniform: participants differed in strategy, political outlook and their understanding of the relationship between diaspora solidarity and African self-government. That diversity is part of its history, not a reason to erase it.

Manchester 1945 and the demand for freedom

The Fifth Pan-African Congress, held in Manchester in October 1945, became a particularly consequential moment. It brought together activists and intellectuals including W. E. B. Du Bois, George Padmore, Kwame Nkrumah and Jomo Kenyatta. The Congress challenged colonialism and racism more directly and helped connect international networks with the emerging independence movements in Africa and the Caribbean.

Its importance should not be exaggerated into a claim that one gathering alone caused decolonisation. Independence struggles were shaped by local political movements, labour organising, women’s activism, veterans returning from war, international pressure and the changing balance of power after the Second World War. But Manchester helped give these struggles a wider language of solidarity and a network through which ideas and strategies could travel.

As independence movements gained momentum, the centre of Pan-African political activity increasingly shifted towards the continent. Ghana's independence in 1957, under Kwame Nkrumah's leadership, gave new force to the argument that sovereignty should be a platform for continental liberation rather than an end in itself. The All-African People's Conference held in Accra in December 1958 further expressed the ambition to connect liberation movements and accelerate the end of colonial rule.

1963: Unity becomes an institution

On 25 May 1963, representatives of 32 independent African states met in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, and established the Organisation of African Unity (OAU). The OAU Charter expressed commitments to African unity and solidarity, cooperation, sovereignty and the eradication of colonialism. It also provided a continental forum through which independent states could coordinate diplomatic positions and support liberation struggles, including the fight against apartheid.

The creation of the OAU was a major institutional achievement, but it was also a compromise among states with different political systems, priorities and views about the pace and form of integration. The debate between immediate political federation and a more gradual approach to cooperation did not disappear. The OAU's emphasis on state sovereignty helped hold the organisation together, while also limiting the extent to which it could intervene in the internal affairs of member states.

By the late twentieth century, the continent faced a different set of challenges. Formal colonial rule had largely ended, but conflict, economic vulnerability, debt, limited industrial capacity and the uneven effects of globalisation remained. The question was no longer only how to defend independence, but how to use it to build prosperity, peace and influence.

From the OAU to the African Union

The Sirte Declaration of 9 September 1999 called for the establishment of an African Union. The Constitutive Act was adopted in Lomé in July 2000 and entered into force on 26 May 2001. The African Union was officially launched in Durban, South Africa, in July 2002 as the successor to the OAU. The transition reflected a renewed emphasis on integration, development, peace and security, democratic governance and Africa's position in the global economy.

The AU's vision of an integrated, prosperous and peaceful Africa, driven by its citizens, carries forward the Pan-African ambition while recognising that unity requires institutions, policies and sustained cooperation. Agenda 2063 later provided a long-term development framework intended to connect continental aspirations with practical priorities across economic transformation, integration, governance and human development.

The unfinished work: unity in practice

History offers inspiration, but it also sets a demanding standard. Pan-Africanism cannot be measured only by commemorations, speeches or the number of organisations carrying the word “African” in their names. Its contemporary value depends on whether it helps solve shared problems and expand the choices available to African people.

Economic integration is one test. The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), which entered into force in 2019 and began trading in January 2021, is designed to create a single market for goods and services and deepen continental economic integration. It is a significant institutional step, but agreements do not automatically produce trade. Businesses need reliable transport and energy, access to finance, predictable customs processes, digital infrastructure, standards, market information and the ability to compete. Workers and communities need to participate in the value created.

Political solidarity is another test. Cooperation is most credible when institutions are accountable, public resources are managed responsibly, human rights are respected and citizens can participate meaningfully in decisions. Continental unity should not mean silence about institutional failures. A mature commitment to Africa requires the courage to address them.

Finally, Pan-Africanism must remain a people's project. Students, entrepreneurs, researchers, artists, farmers, workers, public servants and communities should be more than an audience for continental plans. They must have practical routes to shape them. Regional exchange programmes, research partnerships, cross-border enterprise, shared infrastructure and professional networks can make unity tangible.

A living inheritance

The movement that developed through the 1900 conference, the congresses of the twentieth century, the liberation struggles, the OAU and the African Union was never a finished blueprint. It has been an evolving response to changing historical circumstances. Its enduring lesson is that shared destiny requires organisation: ideas must become institutions, solidarity must become cooperation, and sovereignty must be used to widen human possibility.

The generation inheriting this history should neither romanticise the past nor dismiss it. The task is to understand what earlier generations achieved, learn from the compromises and failures they encountered, and build the next chapter with discipline. The most meaningful tribute to Pan-Africanism is not simply to speak of unity. It is to make cooperation work.
