Beyond Market Size: Making African Trade Work for Enterprise and Shared Prosperity

The AfCFTA offers a historic opportunity to connect African markets. Realising it will depend on productive firms, capable institutions, infrastructure and the ability of smaller businesses to compete.

By Jabes Blondy · 11 November 2026 · 8 min read

Africa's economic future will not be determined by the size of its market alone. It will depend on what the continent can produce, how efficiently businesses can move goods and services, whether firms can grow beyond their first customers and how widely the gains from trade are shared. The African Continental Free Trade Area offers a framework for a more connected market. The harder task is turning that framework into productive opportunity.

A continental agreement with a practical purpose

The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is one of the flagship projects of the African Union's Agenda 2063. The agreement was signed in Kigali on 21 March 2018, entered into force on 30 May 2019 and trading under the AfCFTA regime commenced on 1 January 2021. Its central ambition is to create a single market for goods and services, deepen economic integration and strengthen Africa's position in global trade.

Its significance extends beyond tariff reductions. The agreement includes frameworks for trade in goods and services, rules and procedures for dispute settlement, investment, intellectual property and competition policy. It provides a basis for African economies to connect production systems, develop regional value chains and create larger markets for firms that currently operate within relatively fragmented national markets.

For a small manufacturer, a digital service provider or an agricultural processor, a larger market can create the possibility of scale. A business that has reached the limits of demand in one city or country may find new customers across borders. Suppliers may access more buyers. Producers may have stronger incentives to invest in quality, technology and productivity. These are possibilities, not automatic outcomes.

Trade agreements do not move goods by themselves

A business owner does not experience regional integration as a treaty document. They experience it through the cost, time and uncertainty involved in reaching a customer. A product can qualify for preferential treatment on paper and still struggle to cross a border if documentation is unclear, transport is unreliable, standards are inconsistent or payments are difficult to settle.

For this reason, trade facilitation is as important as tariff liberalisation. Customs procedures need to be predictable. Rules of origin must be understood and applied consistently. Transport corridors, ports, roads, energy systems and digital networks need investment. Regulatory agencies need the capacity to coordinate. Businesses need accessible information about standards, licensing, market access and dispute resolution.

These requirements connect trade policy to the broader quality of economic institutions. A regional market can only function effectively when public agencies are capable, rules are transparent and firms can plan with reasonable confidence. Integration is therefore not solely the responsibility of trade ministries. It also concerns finance, infrastructure, education, technology, competition and governance.

The enterprise challenge: helping firms grow

One of the central questions for African economies is not simply how many businesses are created, but how many become more productive, employ more people and develop the capacity to compete. The World Bank's October 2025 Africa's Pulse report highlights a jobs challenge: many new labour-market entrants work in low-productivity informal activities, while wage-paying employment represents a relatively small share of total employment in Sub-Saharan Africa. The report argues that job creation at scale requires stronger foundations, including infrastructure, skills, a conducive business environment and capable institutions.

That diagnosis matters for the AfCFTA. If firms are unable to grow at home, they may struggle to compete regionally. Small and medium-sized enterprises often face constraints in access to finance, working capital, reliable electricity, digital systems, logistics, market intelligence and skilled labour. They may also lack the documentation and quality systems required to supply larger buyers. Market access is meaningful only when firms can meet the conditions of participation.

There is a role here for business associations, universities, financial institutions, development partners and governments. Practical support can include export-readiness programmes, standards training, digital record-keeping, accessible credit, shared logistics, market research and connections to buyers. Support should not create permanent dependency. Its purpose should be to improve capability and enable firms to operate competitively.

From extraction to value creation

For too long, many African economies have been exposed to the risks of exporting primary commodities while importing higher-value manufactured goods and services. The challenge is not that commodity production has no value; it is that economies need stronger capabilities to process, manufacture, design, distribute and provide services around the resources they possess.

Regional value chains can help. Agricultural products can be processed closer to where they are grown. Inputs for construction, pharmaceuticals, textiles and food production can be sourced from multiple African markets. Digital services can be delivered across borders. Creative industries can reach new audiences. These opportunities require investment, skills, standards and reliable institutions, but they can allow more value to be retained within the continent.

Industrial development must also be environmentally and socially responsible. Expanding production should not mean ignoring resource efficiency, labour standards, environmental protection or the needs of communities. Competitiveness that depends on unsafe work or irreversible environmental damage is not a durable model of prosperity.

Making inclusion part of the design

Small businesses, women-owned enterprises, young entrepreneurs and firms outside major commercial centres should not be treated as an afterthought. If the benefits of integration concentrate only among established firms with the strongest networks, the political and social legitimacy of the project will weaken.

Inclusion requires deliberate attention to information, finance, digital access and the practical barriers that affect smaller firms. It also requires evidence: which businesses are trading, which are unable to participate, where costs remain highest and whether new opportunities are translating into decent work. Policies should be adjusted using what the evidence reveals rather than relying only on broad claims about the size of the market.

The institutional work behind shared prosperity

The AfCFTA is a historic framework, but it is not a substitute for domestic economic reform or enterprise development. Governments must implement commitments, improve trade infrastructure and strengthen institutions. Businesses must invest in quality, productivity, innovation and responsible management. Universities and training institutions must connect learning to productive sectors. Financial institutions must develop products that reflect the realities of growing firms. Regional bodies must make coordination practical.

Shared prosperity emerges when opportunity is matched by capability. The measure of the AfCFTA should therefore not be only the number of countries that have signed or ratified an agreement. It should also be whether a producer can reach a new market, whether a young company can scale, whether a worker can access better employment and whether communities participate in the value generated by regional trade.

A larger African market is an important beginning. The real achievement will be a more productive African economy: one in which firms can grow, people can work with dignity and the gains from integration are broad enough to strengthen confidence in the continental project.
